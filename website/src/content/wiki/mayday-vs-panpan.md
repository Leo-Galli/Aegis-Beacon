# Mayday vs Pan Pan

If you reach rescuers by voice (a PMR446 handheld, a phone, an amateur channel), the words MAYDAY and PAN PAN are not decoration: they tell the receiving side how urgent the situation is.

## The two prefixes

| Prefix | Means | Use when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| MAYDAY | Immediate danger to life | The situation is happening now |
| PAN PAN | Urgent, not immediately life-threatening | You need help but can wait a bit |

MAYDAY is repeated three times (`MAYDAY MAYDAY MAYDAY`). PAN PAN is usually repeated three times too (`PAN PAN PAN`).

## The structure of the call

1. Prefix: `MAYDAY MAYDAY MAYDAY`
2. Who you are: `This is [name/callsign]`
3. Position: in the format the receiver expects (see [Position Reporting](position-reporting))
4. Nature: `One injured skier, possible broken leg, bad weather moving in`
5. Intent: `Remaining in place, transmitting beacon on [frequency]`

## The beacon connection

Your beacon transmission IS the distress signal: it repeats SOS, identity and coordinates without you saying a word. When you reach a human by voice, the call above converts the beacon's automatic message into a structured report, and you can add what the beacon cannot: the nature of the emergency and your intent.

## What not to say

- Do not say "false alarm" explanations on the air; give facts.
- Do not use MAYDAY for an inconvenience: a flat tire on a road is PAN PAN at most.
- Do not stay on the channel longer than the call needs; the channel belongs to the emergency now.
