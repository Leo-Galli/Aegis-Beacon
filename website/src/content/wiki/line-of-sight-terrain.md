# Line of Sight and Terrain

The 15 km figure in the marketing is line-of-sight over flat ground. Real terrain changes everything. This page gives the honest numbers.

## The basic physics

433 MHz is a VHF-ish/UHF band: it needs a clear path. It does not follow the curvature of the earth and it does not bend around ridges. If a rock is between the antennas, there is no signal.

| Terrain | Typical range |
|---------|---------------|
| Flat, clear line of sight, antennas raised | 5-15 km |
| Forest | 1-3 km (foliage loss) |
| Valley with a ridge blocking the path | 0.2-1 km |
| Across a valley, same ridge line (height match) | 3-10 km |
| Beacon buried in snow | 10-100 m depending on depth |

## The mountain rule

In the Alps or any mountainous area, the practical rule is: **you hear what you can see**. If you can see the other side of the valley, the link likely works; if a ridge is in the way, it does not.

## Height is everything

- A beacon at 2000 m talking to a base at 1800 m across a valley: great link.
- The same beacon 50 m lower, behind a shoulder: dead.
- Deploy on high points, put the antenna up, and orient the case so the antenna is vertical.

## Foliage and snow

- Wet leaves absorb 433 MHz noticeably: expect 3-6 dB loss through a treeline.
- Snow has a moderate loss that grows with water content and depth; see the snow behavior page for numbers.

## Planning around it

1. Choose beacon positions with a visible path to where rescuers will be.
2. Place the beacon high, not just "where the victim is".
3. During a search, the SEARCH team should climb to high points to get over blocking terrain.
4. Know the terrain before you deploy; a map study beats radio hope.
