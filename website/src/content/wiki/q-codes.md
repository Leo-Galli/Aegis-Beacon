# Q Codes

Q codes are three-letter abbreviations from the radio-telegraph era that still do real work on the air. A handful are worth knowing for anyone who listens to or reports on the beacon's band.

## The essential set

| Code | Question | Statement |
| --- | --- | --- |
| QTH | What is your position? | My position is... |
| QSY | Shall I change frequency? | Change to... |
| QRM | Are you troubled by interference? | I am troubled by interference |
| QRN | Are you troubled by static? | I am troubled by static |
| QRZ | Who is calling me? | ...is calling you |
| QSL | Do you acknowledge receipt? | I acknowledge receipt |
| QRX | When will you call again? | I will call again at... |
| QSO | Can you communicate? | I can communicate |

## In context

- `QTH 45.8325 N 6.8650 E` is a position report in classic form.
- `QRM` describes man-made interference; `QRN` describes atmospheric noise. The distinction matters when reporting band conditions (see [Interference Sources](interference-sources)).
- `QSL` has drifted into general use to mean "understood, confirmed", which is how you will hear it most often.

## Q codes and the beacon

The beacon's Morse payload does not use Q codes: it sends the payload format documented in [Config Payload Format](config-payload-format). But when you discuss the band with other radio users, Q codes are the shared vocabulary, and using them correctly marks you as someone who knows the medium.
