# Emergency Communications Plan

## Overview

A beacon that nobody monitors is a transmitter, not a rescue plan. Before relying on the Aegis-Beacon in the backcountry, agree with your group and your contacts on who listens, when, and on what frequencies.

## The Listening Problem

The beacon transmits on 433 MHz ISM. Unless someone has a scanner, SDR or a compatible receiver tuned to your frequencies, the signal is only heard by chance. Plan the monitoring side before you need it.

## Plan Components

| Component | Your answer |
|-----------|-------------|
| Frequencies | 433.500 MHz plus one backup (see Frequency Compatibility) |
| Who monitors | A contact with an SDR or scanner, or a mountain hut with radio |
| Schedule | How often the monitor checks |
| Timeout | When they should alert rescue services |
| Group protocol | What each member carries and monitors |

## Home Base Monitoring

1. Leave a second beacon or an SDR in SEARCH/monitor mode at base.
2. Confirm the base unit hears a test transmission from the intended field area.
3. Agree on the expected payload (name + GPS) so an anomaly is obvious.

## Group Protocol

| Member | Role |
|--------|------|
| Beacon carrier | Transmits SOS + name + GPS |
| Companion | Carries a receiver and knows the search patterns |
| Base contact | Monitors frequencies, calls rescue services on timeout |

## Realistic Expectations

- The beacon is one layer. It is not a substitute for a PLB/EPIRB in life-threatening emergencies (see Regulatory Compliance).
- Test the whole chain - transmit, receive, decode, map the coordinates - before the trip, not during it.
