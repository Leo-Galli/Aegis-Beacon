# Ground to Air Signals

A helicopter or fixed-wing search aircraft can read messages written on the ground. The signals are standardized worldwide, which means the crew does not need to guess what your pattern means.

## The standard symbols

| Symbol | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| V | Need assistance |
| X | Need medical assistance |
| Arrow (pointing) | Proceeding in this direction |
| Y | Yes |
| N | No |
| Triangle | Here is the landing direction |
| F | Need food and water |

Build them large enough to be seen from the search altitude: at least 2 - 3 meters per element, with high contrast against the ground (snow, trampled grass, rocks, clothing, or signal panels).

## Making them readable

- Flat, open ground: a signal in a hollow cannot be seen.
- Contrast beats size: dark material on snow, light material on soil.
- Weight the edges so the wind does not scatter them.
- Walk away from the signal; standing in it makes it unreadable.

## In snow

Trampled letters in deep snow work, but fresh snowfall buries them. Re-check and re-trample after every snowfall. The beacon keeps transmitting while you maintain the signals; radio and visual layers together are strongest.

## Helicopter arrival

When the helicopter is landing, the classic signals apply: arms up in a Y shape to signal "yes, land here", one arm up one down for "no, do not land", and stand with your back to the wind, arms spread like a cross, once it is on the ground (crew instructions).
